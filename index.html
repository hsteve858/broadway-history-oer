Course Syllabus & Learning Outcomes

THE 2076 — Course Description

Explores the evolution of musical theatre as a uniquely American theatrical artform. Provides an in-depth understanding of the changing sociopolitical contexts throughout the 19th, 20th, and 21st centuries that directly influenced the trajectory of musical theatre in the American zeitgeist.

Course Outcomes

  1. Identify key performers, directors, writers, producers, and composers who have shaped the trajectory of American musical theatre.
  2. Understand the continually unfolding history of musical theatre beginning in the 19th century through today — specifically the representations of politics, race, class, gender, sexuality, religion, and ability excluded from and inherent in musicals.
  3. Analyze the six primary eras of musical theatre history.
  4. Critically examine the technical advancements born out of musical theatre.
  5. Formulate and apply an argument about a musical's themes, composition, design, performance, and cultural impact.
  6. Create your own production concept based on elements taught throughout the semester.

Setting the Stage

Welcome to Broadway

Welcome to Broadway: The History of the American Musical

In this toolkit, you’ll find information about the history of American musical theatre. It begins with a brief overview of key terms, musical theatre styles, and a breakdown of the musical format. From there, it moves through a comprehensive history of musical theatre beginning in the 19th Century and continuing through the 21st Century. Musical theatre, like all artforms, is shaped by sociopolitical and cultural contexts. The goings-on in the world directly impact how musical theatre transformed and grew into a uniquely American artform.

Musical theatre is as American as baseball, apple pie, and taxes. Audiences flocked to Broadway and regional theatres across the country to experience the extravagant sets, musical numbers, and stars that made this theatrical form what it is today. As musical theatre began to dominate the cultural zeitgeist in the 20's, over 200 productions were set to open on Broadway in a single year!

Throughout the course of the 20th Century, musical theatre became the defining American theatrical form. Composers like the Gershwins, Berlin, Rodgers/Hammerstein, Lerner/Lowe, Bernstein, Sondheim, Kander/Ebb, and Lloyd Webber, revolutionized storytelling through the musical medium. Directors and choreographers like de Mille, Abbott, Robbins, Fosse, and Bennett created some of the most recognizable shows that are continually reproduced and reimagined today. Stars like Waters, Baker, Merman, Lansbury, LuPone, Patinkin, and Peters embodied iconic characters that gripped audiences and cemented their legacies.

From the early extravaganzas like Ziegfeld Follies to the contemporary a cappella production of In Transit, Broadway continues to be the place in which to push the boundaries of musical theatre. Musical theatre lives on as a form which can reflect the American experience back onto audiences; the good, bad, and ugly. American musical theatre has always served as a mirror to society and has been both a space in which marginalized groups have been excluded as well as celebrated. Understanding the historical context in order to appreciate how far musical theatre has come is the purpose of the course.

Intro to Theatre

Foundational Concepts & Vocabulary

Below are concepts and terms to help with distinguishing musical theatre from other performance forms. Understanding key terms and musical styles provides useful context for moving through the six historical eras of musical theatre.

Foundational Concepts

The Golden Triangle

Represents a model in which three elements, music, words, and action, fuse to create the basis for musical theatre performance.

The Bubble Formula

The idea that, in musical theatre, when words can no longer express and contain heightened emotion; the body sings, and when song becomes insufficient, the body dances. Dialogue → Song → Dance.

Key Vocabulary

Composer — the person who writes the music.
Choreography — the dance steps or movement on stage, created by the choreographer.
Libretto (book) — the story, characters, and script, written by the librettist.
Lyrics — the words to the songs, written by the lyricist.
Score — the music and lyrics together, written by the composer.

Types of Musicals

Book Musical

Traditional musical form in which the libretto is integrated with song and dance to tell a linear narrative. Popular “The Golden Age” examples include Wicked, Chicago, My Fair Lady, & West Side Story. .

Rock / Pop-Rock Musical

Modern musical built upon a contemporary music aesthetic like hip hop, rock, and pop. Themes often include counterculture, youth rebellion, alienation, etc. Examples: Hair, RENT, Spring Awakening, Jagged Little Pill, American Idiot, Hamilton.

Concept Musical

Modern musicals in which the theme or idea is more important than plot; songs and dances are connected by a theme to convey an idea instead of relaying a linear story. Sondheim’s musicals are a prime example of this.

Sung-Through Musical

Modern musical form which resembles an opera by being mostly sung and underscored with little spoken dialogue. Examples include works by Boublil and Schönberg and Andrew Lloyd Webber

Jukebox Musical

A musical consisting of a series of pre-written songs (typically popular hits) connected into a loose storyline. Can be from one or multiple composers, including pop artists and music groups. Examples: Mamma Mia!, Jersey Boys, Rock of Ages, & Juliet, The Cher Show.

Dance Musical

A musical that is almost entirely danced, with little or no spoken dialogue and often a jukebox score. Examples: Fosse, Contact, Movin' Out, Come Fly Away.

Reflexive Musical

A post-Modern category of self-referential musicals that celebrate and/or poke fun at their own musical identity. Examples: Urinetown, The Producers, Monty Python's Spamalot, Death Becomes Her.

Screen-to-Stage Musical

A musical using the book and some/all of the score of an existing film. Examples: Beauty and the Beast, The Color Purple, Billy Elliot, Mary Poppins, Newsies, Hairspray. Disney especially dominates this category.

18th Century — 1900s

The Antecedents

Although musical theatre is a uniquely American invention, its roots extend all the way back to the 18th Century and even across the Atlantic back to Europe.

Two of the most famous “pre-musical theatre” artists were William Gilbert and Arthur Sullivan of Gilbert & Sullivan fame! Their work in bringing light opera to stages across the United Kingdom and beyond helped inspire the theatrical performances underway in the United States at the end of the 19th Century.

Minstrelsy

Like most American artforms, musical theatre has a checkered history. Originally, the bulk of live musical performances in the US were being performed as Minstrelsy/minstrel shows. These were Blackface performances that date back to the late 1700s and continued all the way through to the early 20th Century. Unfortunately, there are still Blackface performances occurring today (such as in the film Tropic Thunder).

1828 — Thomas D. Rice creates the "Jim Crow" character.
1843 — The Virginia Minstrels, a blackface group, form in NYC.
1846 — The Christy Minstrels form in NYC and introduce key minstrelsy characters like the Interlocutor.

These shows depicted derogatory, racist stereotypes of Southern slaves, and also derided immigrants, women, and other marginalized groups. The form was extremely popular in the U.S. from 1850–1870, performed by and for white, male audiences, and followed a specific structure:

  1. Preamble — walk-around / cakewalk
  2. Mr. Interlocutor and Endmen (Mr. Tambo and Bones)
  3. "Olio" — variety acts
  4. "Afterpiece" — one-act play or sketch show
  5. Celebratory dance

A recent musical depicting the minstrel show structure is Kander and Ebb's, The Scottsboro Boys (2010).

After the Civil War, African American performers began to form their own minstrel groups, thus reclaiming the performance form:

1865 -- Booker and Clayton’s Georgia Minstrels was the first popular African American blackface minstrel troupe.
1891 — The Creole Show: all-Black cast, and the first female interlocutor.
1898 — Clorindy, or the Origin of the Cakewalk: a one-act musical and the first Broadway musical with an all-Black cast.

The legacy of the Minstrel Show is one full of racist and bigoted views while also introducing some popular musical forms like: Gospel, The Blues, Tap dancing, and the two-performer comedy routine.

Other 19th-Century Forms

Burlesque

Satirical, musical productions. Oftentimes spoofed famous works like Shakespeare and engaged in social commentary. 1868:Lydia Thompson and “British Blondes” bring burlesque to America Parodies, variety acts, drag, improv, risque costumes.

Extravaganza

Similar to burlesque but with less satire and more spectacle, containing a variety of performances.

Vaudeville

A series of unrelated acts (a variety show), with origins in New York City's Bowery saloons — known as "immigrant entertainment."

Black Vaudeville

The T.O.B.A. (Theatre Owners Booking Association) circuit formed in the 1910s specifically for Black performers and audiences. Josephine Baker, Ma Rainey, Bessie Smith, and Louis Armstrong got their start on this circuit.

Early 20th Century

Hammerstein Builds a Neighborhood

In the late 1890’s, Oscar Hammerstein bought a plot of land between 43rd and 45th Streets in New York City and began building theaters. Broadway as we know it was no more than a simple street at this time, but Hammerstein’s presence supercharged this area of Midtown Manhattan. In 1904, the NYC Subway opened for operation which helped bring much needed foot traffic and business to this area of town. By 1910, 34 theaters were established and running in and around Midtown. In addition to Hammerstein I, there were other influential figures helping shape the early days of Broadway.

Florenz Ziegfeld Jr. 1867–1932

Along with his wife, Anna Held, created the Ziegfeld Follies in 1907 — an annual extravaganza he produced until 1931. "The Ziegfeld Girls" were the first chorus dancers on Broadway and direct inspiration for The Rockettes.

George M. Cohan 1878–1942

Dubbed "the man who owned Broadway," Cohan wrote, composed, produced, and appeared in over three dozen Broadway shows. Helped develop the "book musical" by advancing plot within the libretto and score, and was an instrumental part of Tin Pan Alley.

Jerome Kern 1885–1945

Dubbed "The Father of American Musical Theatre," Kern proved that musicals could be more than comedies — that they could address highly prevalent social themes. His innovations include:

  • Overture — the music before the show begins
  • Reprise — a repeat of an earlier song, sometimes reformatted
  • AABA song format — a chorus organized into three repeated melodic refrains
  • Leitmotifs — short underscoring refrains associated with a character or dramatic action — a character's theme song

Major productions: Eddie, Oh, Boy!, Sunny, Show Boat.

In addition to Broadway,"Showboats" were seeing a boom in popularity. These were floating theaters that sailed along the Ohio and Mississippi Rivers. Highly popular from 1831-1940s. Mainly featured minstrel shows, vaudeville, and melodramas. They also inspired the 1927 breakout musical “Showboat” by Jerome Kern.

Roughly 1920 – 1940

The Jazz Age

From roughly 1920-1940, this era of musical theatre (and music in America more broadly) serves as a musical and entertainment revolution. The entire popular music industry becomes integrated into Broadway, Hollywood, and Tin Pan Alley.

Tin Pan Alley was located specifically on West 28th Street between Broadway Ave. and 6th Ave. It was the hot spot for composers, lyricists, and songwriters to churn out popular hits that would spread across the country. Many musical theatre artists came in and out of this highly creative block in NYC. The role of the “song plugger” is formed as well. Some musical forms born from Tin Pan Alley include:

  • Waltzes
  • Sentimental ballads
  • Songs written to be performed in blackface by white performers
  • Ragtime
  • Novelty songs like “take me out to the ball game”

    • The Harlem Renaissance

    During this period, the Harlem Renaissance greatly influenced arts and culture in New York City and around the country. It began the slow process of changing bigoted views toward the African American community. Although, there were frequent tensions between the predominantly Black performances occurring in Harlem and the predominantly white shows produced on Broadway.

    1917 — A series of plays rejecting Blackface and minstrelsy are produced in Harlem.
    1920s–30s — Height of the Harlem Renaissance, ushering in a new wave of African American culture, fashion, literature, politics, art, and music.

    Popular figures from the time include Langston Hughes, W.E.B. Du Bois, Marcus Garvey, Duke Ellington, Ethel Waters, and Josephine Baker.

    Important Figures of the Era

    Irving Berlin 1888–1989

    A remarkably prolific songwriter who composed 899 songs, 451 of them hits. Wrote Annie Get Your Gun as well as the song "White Christmas." Major productions: Annie Get Your Gun, Face the Music, As Thousands Cheer.

    George Gershwin 1898–1937

    Emerged from Tin Pan Alley to write the modern masterpiece "Rhapsody in Blue," and worked with his brother Ira on classics like Porgy and Bess. An early example of mixing classical music with jazz and the blues, he helped integrate jazz into Broadway. Major productions: Of Thee I Sing, Porgy and Bess, Lady Be Good!, Strike Up the Band, Let 'Em Eat Cake.

    Ira Gershwin 1896–1983

    Co-wrote the lyrics for Porgy and Bess and moved to Hollywood with George to work on film scores. Hits include "I Got Rhythm," "'S Wonderful," and "Embraceable You."

    Cole Porter 1891–1964

    Wrote over 800 songs and musicals, including Anything Goes and Kiss Me, Kate, winning several Tony Awards.

    1943 – 1964  ·  Oklahoma! to Fiddler on the Roof

    The Golden Age

    The Golden Age era is argued to have lasted roughly from 1943–1964. Scholars say it is bookended by the musicals Oklahoma! and Fiddler on the Roof. Many elements of musical theatre help define this prolific era in musical theatre history including the expansion of the libretto and a stronger focus on choreography. In addition, the director and choreographer are both now seen as integral to a successful musical (not so much leading up to this era).

    While all art reflects the era from which it emerges, the Golden Age of musical theatre sees a specific focus on musicals about the American identity including themes more explicitly about race, gender, sexuality, youth culture, and corporatism. Naturally, the post-WWII period has an extremely important influence on musical theatre. The theatre becomes a place of refuge and, especially, entertainment. But Broadway does not solely focus on American identities, it also welcomes stories about international identities and struggles (Rodgers and Hammerstein have a huge influence in this area). Female characters become important figures of strength and self-determination and musical theatre begins to expand the concept of gender roles. It is also in this era that the industry’s biggest award ceremony is introduced: the Tony Awards (1947).

    Rodgers & Hammerstein and Their Circle

    Oscar Hammerstein II 1895–1960

    Son of Hammerstein I, creator of the theatre district. Massively influential as a lyricist, worked on 11 musicals with Richard Rodgers. R&H became famous for writing a vast array of stories that both dealt with the American experience as well as international stories. His innovations include:

    • Quasi Love Songs: romance suggested rather than blatantly announced
    • Self-contained song/scene: action/resolution of a scene in song format
    • Song-as-soliloquy: one character singing their thoughts aloud
    • Metatheater: a show within a show. (This has been around for centuries but Hammerstein was one of the first to really integrate it into musical theatre

    Major productions: Oklahoma!, The King and I, Cinderella, The Sound of Music, Show Boat, Flower Drum Song.

    Richard Rodgers 1902–1979

    Born into a musical, Jewish family; Jerome Kern was a huge influence. His primary collaborators were first Hart, then Hammerstein II. With Hart, he formed the first writing duo nicknamed a "team." Major productions: Oklahoma!, The Sound of Music, Cinderella, Flower Drum Song, The King and I, Pal Joey.

    Lorenz Hart 1895–1943

    A "self-expressive" lyricist writing from lived experience, Hart's lyrics presented love in carnal, cynical, and satirical terms — helping to define the Golden Age. His partnership with Rodgers ended on a sour note when Hart showed up drunk to opening night of their final project, A Connecticut Yankee.

    Ethel Waters 1896–1977

    Began singing in local nightclubs at 17 and worked the Chitlin' Circuit — segregated venues featuring Black performers. In 1920 she moved to Harlem and contributed to the Harlem Renaissance, famously distrusting "white theatre." Made her Broadway debut in Africana (1927); in 1933 she starred in As Thousands Cheer, the only Black actor in the cast, delivering a famously heart-wrenching performance.

    Directors & Choreographers

    George Abbott 1887–1995

    Involved with over 100 shows across a 70-year career as actor, playwright, director, and producer. Major productions: Pal Joey, On the Town, The Pajama Game, Damn Yankees, A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum.

    Jerome Robbins 1918–1998

    Started with the Yiddish Art Theatre and became a member of the Ballet Theatre (with Agnes de Mille). Solidified the Golden Triangle concept by reemphasizing dance's role, and was the first director/choreographer combo — inspiring many more to follow. Major productions: The King and I, Fiddler on the Roof, West Side Story, Gypsy, On the Town.

    Agnes de Mille 1905–1993

    Born to a prosperous show-business family; helped popularize "character dances" combining pantomime and formal dance, and helped build the American Ballet Theatre with Robbins. Collaborated with Rodgers & Hammerstein (Oklahoma!, Carousel) and Lerner & Loewe (Brigadoon, Allegro).

    Dorothy Fields 1904–1974

    The "Woman Songwriter," she made her start in Tin Pan Alley before moving to Hollywood — the first woman to win both an Oscar and a Tony. Co-wrote over 400 songs, 15 musicals, and 26 films, including the book for Annie Get Your Gun, and wrote songs for Black performers during the Harlem Renaissance.

    Alan Jay Lerner 1918–1986

    Met his future partner Loewe at a NYC social club. Wrote lighthearted shows with social themes, and unintentionally wrote the first concept musical, Love Life, with Kurt Weill. Major productions: My Fair Lady, Brigadoon, Camelot, Paint Your Wagon.

    Frederick Loewe 1904–1988

    Born in Berlin, considered a musical prodigy by age 7; immigrated to the US in 1924 and didn't find success for a decade. My Fair Lady was the most successful Broadway show since Oklahoma! and cemented Lerner and Loewe as Broadway icons.

    The Pygmalion Myth

    The idea that a woman can be molded into a male-dominated ideal — that she cannot reach her full potential without a man's intervention. Explored in My Fair Lady and seen throughout Oklahoma!, Kiss Me, Kate, The Sound of Music, Gypsy, Funny Girl, and later in Grease, Hairspray, 42nd Street, Legally Blonde, and most fairy tales — a defining feature of the Golden Age.

    Audre Lorde (1934–1992), though not a musical theatre artist, wrote extensively on gender, sexuality, race, feminism, and intersectionality (a term she helped popularize), influencing the sociocultural landscape of the period — including her seminal 1984 essay "The Master's Tools Will Never Dismantle the Master's House."

    Jule Styne 1905–1994

    The most produced composer of original musicals during the Golden Age — from 1949–1974, a Styne-scored musical premiered on Broadway every season. An EGOT winner known for writing star vehicles for performers like Carol Channing, Mary Martin, Ethel Merman, Barbra Streisand, and Carol Burnett. Major productions: Gypsy, Funny Girl, Peter Pan, Gentlemen Prefer Blondes.

    Ethel Merman 1908–1984

    The "undisputed First Lady of the musical comedy stage." Starred in five Cole Porter musicals, including Reno Sweeney in Anything Goes, and in Annie Get Your Gun, which ran three years — she missed only two performances in the whole run. Major productions: Annie Get Your Gun, Girl Crazy, Hello, Dolly!, Gypsy, Call Me Madam (Tony Award).

    Hooking the Audience

    Techniques for keeping an audience's attention, both codified and popularized during the Golden Age:

    1. Star Vehicle

    A show designed to showcase a particular star's talents. Popular examples: Hello, Dolly!, Fiddler on the Roof, Mame, Evita, Phantom of the Opera, Chicago, Sweeney Todd, Wicked, Hamilton, Hadestown.

    2. The "I Am / I Want" Song

    An early number where a character tells the audience who they are, what they want, and why we should care. Examples: "I Cain't Say No" (Oklahoma!), "I Feel Pretty" (West Side Story), "Some People" (Gypsy), "If I Were a Rich Man" (Fiddler on the Roof), "The Schuyler Sisters" (Hamilton).

    3. Show Opener / Grabber

    The first number, introducing plot, exposition, and main players with a burst of energy. Examples: "Willkommen" (Cabaret), "Rent" (RENT), "Circle of Life" (The Lion King), "Hello!" (The Book of Mormon).

    4. Intermission Teaser

    A powerhouse cliffhanger number right before intermission. Examples: "Everything's Coming Up Roses" (Gypsy), "Don't Rain on My Parade" (Funny Girl), "Defying Gravity" (Wicked).

    5. The 11:00 Number

    A dramatic high note late in the show — named for when it would occur in an 8pm curtain, 3-hour show. Examples: "Rose's Turn" (Gypsy), "Being Alive" (Company), "You Can't Stop the Beat" (Hairspray), "One" (A Chorus Line).

    Jewish Influence in Musical Theatre

    By 1910, nearly a quarter of the New York City population identified as Jewish. Seeing as Broadway was the epicenter for musical theatre, this also meant that Jewish people had a significant influence on the artform. Indeed, many Jewish neighborhoods in NYC were sites of intense creative generation, such as Tin Pan Alley.

    This also meant that there was a distinct Yiddish theatre as well as English-speaking theater emerging throughout NYC. Although the Jewish identity was pronounced among musical theatre creatives, many sought to change their names to sound “less-Jewish” for fear of persecution. Due to shared struggles and persecution (especially during WWII), Jewish presence in musical theatre also led to an increased presence and acceptance of African Americans on Broadway and beyond.

    West Side Story and Its Legacy

    West Side Story opened in 1957 and, despite running only 732 performances, inspired a film that garnered 10 Oscar nominations and 9 wins. It lost the Tony for Best Musical to The Music Man — a stark contrast in tone. It introduced the concept of the triple threat performer (sing, act, dance), and though it centered Puerto Rican immigrants, it was created by four Jewish men — a fact that continues to complicate its legacy.

    Leonard Bernstein 1918–1990

    A celebrated symphony conductor and composer who crossed over into musical theatre, bringing a classical complexity to his compositions. His Young People's Concerts elevated the "conductor" to a fame status and taught music to kids nationwide. Collaborated with Robbins, Sondheim, Lerner, Comden, and Green. Major productions: On the Town, Wonderful Town, Candide, West Side Story.

    Stephen Sondheim 1930–2021

    Without question the most important, prolific, and celebrated composer/lyricist in American musical theatre. Mentored by Oscar Hammerstein II, he fell in love with musical theatre after seeing Hammerstein's Very Warm for May and, by 16, had written his first show. West Side Story was his first Broadway show, as lyricist. His first as composer/lyricist, A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum (1962), remains his biggest hit, running 964 performances. He popularized the concept musical with Company.

    Career highlights:

    • Created 19 musicals (16 as sole composer/lyricist)
    • 8 Tony Awards — more than any other composer
    • Pulitzer Prize for Sunday in the Park with George
    • 1 Academy Award, 8 Grammy Awards, Kennedy Center Honors

    Frequent performers of his work include Bernadette Peters, Patti LuPone, Angela Lansbury, and Mandy Patinkin.

    Harold "Hal" Prince 1928–2019

    A hugely influential producer and director who worked with nearly every major composer, lyricist, and choreographer of the era, and helped develop the "concept musical" through his partnerships with Sondheim. Major productions: West Side Story, Fiddler on the Roof, Cabaret, Sweeney Todd, Evita, Phantom of the Opera.

    The Bookend: Fiddler on the Roof (1964)

    Its themes of Jewish identity, family, tradition, and persecution resonated deeply, and it became the first musical to run over 3,000 performances — winning 9 Tony Awards including Best Musical Score, Book, Direction, and Choreography.

    Sheldon Harnick 1924–2023

    Served in WWII writing USO songs. Worked closely with Jerry Bock and Joseph Stein. Major productions: Fiorello!, She Loves Me, Fiddler on the Roof.

    Jerry Bock 1928–2010

    A gifted pianist who wrote for television revues and summer resorts before co-writing Fiddler on the Roof with Harnick and Stein.

    Joseph Stein 1912–2010

    Began in TV writing for Jackie Gleason and Mel Brooks. Major productions: Fiddler on the Roof, Lend Me an Ear, Enter Laughing.

    Zero Mostel 1915–1977

    Won Tonys for both his musicals, A Funny Thing Happened on the Way to the Forum and Fiddler on the Roof. Blacklisted by HUAC for refusing to name other artists during the Red Scare — creating high tension with Robbins (who had cooperated) during Fiddler, though the two put differences aside to create a memorable production.

    1964 – 1999

    Post-Golden Age

    The tumultuous 1960s produce art that responds to an upheaval of American culture and politics.

    It is fitting that the Golden Age should end, in effect, in 1964 with the opening of Fiddler on the Roof.

    A Country in Upheaval

    1961 — Freedom Rides and sit-ins
    1963 — March on Washington; assassination of JFK
    ~1966 — National Organization for Women (NOW) and second-wave feminism
    1968 — Assassinations of MLK Jr. and RFK
    June 1969 — Stonewall Riots
    July 1969 — Moon Landing
    August 1969 — Woodstock

    All of this shaped musical theatre — most apparent in the tribal love-rock musical Hair (1968), created by Gerome Ragni, James Rado, and Galt MacDermot. Anti-war, anti-establishment, pro-drugs, pro-sex, humanist and tribal in sentiment, it produced pop hits like "Aquarius" and "Let the Sunshine In," and helped introduce rock/pop music to Broadway. It was initially produced through Joseph Papp's new concept for NYC theatre: Off-Broadway, a group of small Manhattan theaters (100–499 seats) offering experimental fare with more content flexibility. Other shows that began Off-Broadway: The Threepenny Opera, The Fantasticks, Little Shop of Horrors, Hamilton, Hadestown.

    Kander & Ebb, and the Concept Musical

    John Kander born 1927

    Born in Missouri; one of his first NYC gigs was as rehearsal pianist for West Side Story and accompanist for Gypsy auditions.

    Fred Ebb 1928–2004

    Wrote nightclub songs and theatrical revues before meeting Kander. Together they wrote 17 musicals with a unique ability to write songs that sounded authentic to their setting — "Tomorrow Belongs to Me" from Cabaret was often mistaken for a traditional German folk song. Major productions: Cabaret, Chicago, Kiss of the Spider Woman, The Scottsboro Boys.

    Bob Fosse 1927–1987

    Born in Chicago; started dancing at 9. Popularized the self-reflexive choreographer — dance that calls attention to the performer/choreographer, somewhat opposite de Mille and Robbins — using hats, gloves, and feathers throughout his work. Choreographed 12 Broadway musicals and directed 7, winning 9 Tony Awards. Major productions: The Pajama Game, Sweet Charity, Pippin, Chicago, Cabaret.

    Metatheatre & the Alienation Effect

    Metatheatre: shows that illustrate and celebrate the world of theatre — rehearsal segments, auditions, or a show-within-a-show (Cabaret, A Chorus Line, The Producers). Conceptually metaphoric: works that emphasize that everything is a performance (Cabaret, Hair, Chicago, Pippin). Alienation effect: theatrical elements laid bare for the audience — a term popularized by Bertolt Brecht, who believed theatre should inspire action rather than "suspend disbelief."

    Themes in Pop/Rock Musicals

    • Youth culture — coming-of-age stories, often with younger casts (Spring Awakening)
    • Counterculture — characters alienated from society, bound by lifestyle or misfit status (RENT)
    • Rebellion in search of identity — characters testing boundaries around race, ethnicity, sexuality, gender (Dreamgirls)
    Michael Bennett 1943–1987

    Created dance-centric musicals with moving sets highlighting complex choreography. In 1976, A Chorus Line was nominated for 10 Tonys and won 9, plus a Pulitzer for Drama — at only 32, Bennett became a Broadway institution. Major productions: Company, Follies, A Chorus Line, Dreamgirls.

    Decade by Decade

    • Jesus Christ Superstar (1971) — Webber/Rice711 performances
    • Two Gentlemen of Verona (1971) — Tony Award, Best Musical627 performances
    • The Rocky Horror Show (1975)now a cult classic
    • The Wiz (1975) — all-Black cast1,672 performances
    Stephen Schwartz born 1948

    Began composing for Pippin at Carnegie Mellon. By 26, had three shows running on Broadway at once: Pippin, Godspell, and The Magic Show. Winner of 3 Oscars, 3 Grammys — still no Tony. Major productions: Pippin, Godspell, The Prince of Egypt, Wicked.

    Andrew Lloyd Webber born 1948

Webber popularized the mega-musical and revolutionized Broadway and the West End as a capitalist enterprise: The Phantom of the Opera holds the record for longest-running Broadway production at 13,981 performances. Major productions: Jesus Christ Superstar, Cats, The Phantom of the Opera, Evita, Sunset Boulevard.

    2000 – 2019

    The New Millennium

    In Act II of RENT, Roger sings “you’re living in America, at the end of the millennium, you’re living in America, where it’s like the twilight zone” and that certainly feels apt regarding the immense changes that occur in American culture after 9/11 and the digital age. Much of this New Millennium of musical theatre, and performance more broadly, is still being written. This section looks at the first two decades of the 21st Century, the iconic productions that dominate show business, the creatives that influenced musical theatre, and what changed (or didn’t) after the year 2020.

    Without question, musical theatre in the 2000s became more inclusive both in the stories it told and the people telling them. For much of the 20th Century, musical theatre was dominated by white, cis, mostly Jewish men. However, as culture became more accepting and diversity became seen as a benefit rather than a drawback, musical theatre reflected that shift in the shows that were produced on Broadway, the West End, tours, and regional theatre. And as the New Millennium brings about advancements in technology and culture, musical theatre continues to reflect on its history, interrogate its roots, and influence culture around the world. Below is an incomplete list of select musicals, creatives, themes, and stars that had a huge impact on musical theatre.

    Queer Identity on Broadway

    In today’s world, it may seem hard to imagine that musical theatre was not always open to queer artists or stories but that was the case throughout much of the 20th Century. Many creatives, like Bernstein and Hart, hid their sexuality from the public and carried on relationships with men in private. Yet, as the 20th Century came to a close, musical theatre artists began bringing queer stories to Broadway and beyond. What precipitated this shift and how did it come to be?.

  • After WWII, a more homogenous view of America took hold on Broadway and in Hollywood. Musical theatre creators wrote stories about the White, Anglo-Saxon, Protestant world (think Lerner/Loewe musicals). However, after the Stonewall Riots in 1969 and the Sexual Revolution, activists pushed for more queer representation (which meant cis white gay men in the 70s and 80s).
  • As is the case in a Capitalist society, producers saw the potential to make money off musicals that represented minority groups and thereby tapping into a new market.
  • Although, portraying gay and queer characters on stage is nothing new (especially men in drag), creators began writing gay characters with more depth and story
      • 1975: A Chorus Line premiers which features an openly gay character who gives a powerful and heart-wrenching monologue.
  • Jerry Herman supercharged this with his show La Cage Aux Folles in 1983.
      • Often deemed the “first Broadway homosexual musical” La Cage presents the two leading roles as gay men navigating their relationship.
      It was groundbreaking for its time but still left a lot to be desired by upholding classic gay stereotypes, the two gay men never sharing a kiss, and the story revolving around a heterosexual wedding. Indeed, the chorus is comprised of performers in drag but does little to move forward the art of drag or even comment on trans identity
  • Sadly, the HIV/AIDS epidemic severely complicates the portrayal of queer characters because creators are unsure of how audiences will react to the themes or struggles therein. The reactionary view of HIV/AIDS in America makes the public far more judgmental of queer identity in popular entertainment.
  • Although the HIV/AIDS crisis continued throughout the 90s, stories about queer people broke through. Gay characterizations were more realistic and less stereotypical than in the previous two decades.
      • - Sexuality no longer became the defining trait of characters.
      - Work emerges that dramatizes stories of individuals living with AIDS
      - Portrayals of lesbians finally make it to Broadway
  • Musicals about the HIV/AIDS epidemic also emerge. Some are deeply touching, others are wryly and darkly comedic. These were ways of coping with a disease that ravaged the musical theatre community.
      • - Elegies for Angels, Punks, and Raging Queens (1989)
      - An Unfinished Song (1991)
      - Falsettos (1992)
      - All That He Was (1993)
      - AIDS - The Musical (1995)

    Contemporary Musical Theatre

    This Is the Current Era

    Post-COVID, the Digital Age, and short-form vertical video currently define this ever-evolving moment. Broadway closed on March 12, 2020, and remained shuttered for the rest of the year; even with the 2021 vaccine rollout, it took time before audiences confidently gathered again. One major shake-up came in 2023, when The Phantom of the Opera — Broadway's longest-running show — announced its closing. Shows have since struggled to turn a profit, and Broadway workers have pushed for fairer treatment from unions and producers.

    With the rise of TikTok and Instagram Reels, musical theatre is adapting. One innovative example born out of the 2020 lockdown was the online, crowd-sourced Ratatouille the Musical — a glimpse of how the form might intertwine with social media going forward.

    Musical theatre is an ongoing experiment: this toolkit has offered a quick overview of its history — good, bad, and ugly — highlighting successes, interrogating failures, and looking to the past in order to imagine the future.

    Practical Assignments

    Putting It Into Practice

    1

    Core Viewing Assignments (CVA)

    Choose as many musicals as you wish, assign viewing to students. Ask them to write a 1-page response utilizing at least 5 terms from that unit/musical.

    2

    Response to a Live Musical

    Basically, an expansion of the CVAs. Expand the amount of terms required for this response. Required in-person viewing of a musical.

    3

    Mini Group Performance

    In groups of 3-4, students pick a song from any musical and stage it using elements from the Golden Triangle. The song can be performed as a lip-sync or live with an accompaniment (depending on experience level and space)! Incorporate 3 terms from any unit in the semester.

    4

    Musical Jigsaw Production Concept

    Throughout the semester, students will identify 4 elements from 4 different musicals that appeal to them, these could be a specific choreographer, designer, director, performer, adaptation, etc. Students then create a production concept for their own musical. This could be something totally original or an adaptation/revival of an established musical. Create a slideshow “pitch” to producers (fellow classmates) and include pictures and video.
